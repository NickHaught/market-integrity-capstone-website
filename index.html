Abstract

Prediction markets let people bet on future events, and the price reflects how likely the crowd thinks something is. Platforms like Polymarket and Kalshi have made these markets mainstream for forecasting elections, policy, and world events. The problem is that in thin markets, bad actors can game the system through wash trading, pump-and-dump schemes, and other manipulation.

We built a simulation and detection framework that models these behaviors and tests different detection algorithms against them. The system uses a Mesa-based agent model with a continuous double auction order book, graph-based wash trade detection reformulated as an optimization problem, and transient response analysis from control theory to measure how much manipulation distorts the forecast. Branch and Bound outperformed traditional dynamic programming in both speed and accuracy, and is the only approach that meets a real-time budget.

Background

Prediction markets have been around in various forms since the 1800s. The core idea is simple: aggregate what a bunch of people think into one number. The price of a contract is the implied probability of an event happening.

Wash trading means trading with yourself or coordinated partners to create fake volume. No real ownership changes hands, but the market looks more active and the price gets pushed. Pump-and-dump is when a group buys aggressively to inflate the price, then sells once others pile in. These have been documented in political betting markets [1], and detection is still fragmented even in traditional finance [2]. Prediction markets are thinner and more event-driven, which makes it harder to catch.

Methods

Three main components: a simulated market, graph-based detection, and transient response analysis.

Agent-Based Model

Built in Mesa with a continuous double auction order book. The market has noise traders, informed traders, liquidity providers, wash trade rings, and pump-and-dump coalitions. Everything is parameterizable.

Graph-Based Detection

We build directed graphs from the trade log and look for self-loops and dense cycle structures. We reformulated wash trade detection as a subset matching problem and compared dynamic programming, ILP, and Branch and Bound.

Transient Response

We treat the market like a feedback system and measure how it responds to manipulation shocks. Same metrics as characterizing a fault on a power grid: overshoot, settling time, impedance.

Detection Speed vs Recovery

BnB catches it in ~4 ticks, overshoot stays at 1.9%. DP takes ~20 ticks, overshoot triples to 5.1%. No detection at all: 8.1% overshoot, 80+ ticks to recover. Detection latency directly controls how much damage gets done.

Takeaway

In prediction markets, price is probability. Manipulation doesn't just inflate volume, it corrupts the forecast. Reformulating detection as an optimization problem makes it faster and more accurate, and that speed directly reduces how much the forecast gets messed up.
